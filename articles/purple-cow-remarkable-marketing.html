Purple Cow: Why Being Remarkable Beats Being Better (Seth Godin)

By the Mindfluence team · Updated November 2026 · Reading time: about 7 minutes

Imagine driving through the countryside and passing field after field of brown cows. After a while, you stop noticing them entirely. Now imagine one of those cows is purple. You would stop the car, take a photo, and tell everyone about it. That is the whole argument of Seth Godin’s book Purple Cow, published in 2003: in a world drowning in choice, being slightly better than average is worthless, because average is invisible. The only strategy that works is being remarkable — literally, worth making a remark about. Two decades later, with more products, more ads, and more noise than ever, the argument has only grown stronger.

Key Facts at a Glance

  • Purple Cow is a book by marketing writer Seth Godin, first published in 2003, subtitled “Transform Your Business by Being Remarkable.”
  • A “purple cow” is a product or service so unusual that people notice it and talk about it — remarkable in the literal sense of being worth remarking on.
  • Godin’s core argument: safe, average products are invisible, so playing it safe is now the riskiest strategy a business can follow.
  • Marketing, in his view, must be built into the product itself — not bolted on afterwards with advertising.
  • Remarkable products spread by word of mouth, carried by the small group of enthusiastic early customers Godin calls “sneezers.”

What Is a Purple Cow?

Godin opens the book with a drive through the French countryside, where he noticed that cows, however beautiful, become boring after the twentieth field. Then he asks the reader to imagine a purple cow. The point is not that your product should be purple, or weird for the sake of weirdness. The point is that attention is scarce, and ordinary things do not earn it.

A purple cow is anything that makes a stranger stop, look, and tell someone else. It could be a product feature nobody else offers, a service so generous it surprises people, or a design so distinctive it photographs well. What matters is not the category but the effect: people remark on it, and their remarks do the marketing for you.

This is a sharp break from the old model of marketing, where a company made an average product and then bought attention with advertising. Godin’s argument is that the old model stopped working because the world changed: consumers see far more messages than they can process, and they have learned to ignore almost all of them. An average product with a large advertising budget is now a recipe for being ignored at great expense.

Why Did Godin Say Advertising Stopped Working?

Godin’s case rests on a simple observation: there is too much of everything. In the middle of the twentieth century, a new product had few competitors, television reached almost everyone, and an advertisement could reliably create demand. By 2003, that world was gone. Shelves were full, channels had multiplied, and consumers had developed powerful filters.

The book argues that the economics of attention flipped. When attention was cheap and plentiful, interrupting people with ads made sense. When attention became scarce and expensive, interruption stopped paying. People skip ads, mute commercials, install ad blockers, and scroll past sponsored posts without a second thought. The more money a company spends shouting, the more practised consumers become at not listening.

Godin does not argue that advertising is dead. He argues that it only works for products that are already remarkable — and for those products, advertising is barely needed, because customers do the spreading themselves. The implication is uncomfortable for large companies: the marketing budget should be spent on making the product better, not on telling people about a product that is not worth telling anyone about. Since the book was published, the trend has only accelerated: social media made word of mouth faster, while ad fatigue grew worse.

What Does “Remarkable” Actually Mean?

Godin is careful to define his terms. Remarkable does not mean the best, the most expensive, or the most polished. It means worth making a remark about. A product can be remarkable because it is unusually cheap, unusually fast, unusually generous, unusually beautiful, unusually simple, or unusually strange. The common thread is that it gives people a reason to talk.

This is why being “a bit better” does not count. A slightly faster laptop or a slightly cheaper detergent gives nobody a reason to mention the product to a friend. Remarkability requires a difference large enough to be worth describing: if your customers would not tell a friend about your product unprompted, it is not remarkable yet.

Remarkability is also relative. What counts as remarkable depends on what everyone else in your market is doing. In an industry where every company hides its pricing, transparent pricing is remarkable. In a market where every hotel charges for extras, a hotel that includes everything is remarkable. The purple cow is not a fixed set of features — it is whatever stands out against the particular herd around it.

Finally, remarkability has to be aimed at the right people. Godin argues that the mass market is a poor target for a new product, because it is cautious and hard to reach. The right audience is the small group of enthusiastic early adopters — the “sneezers” — who love new things, try them first, and tell everyone they know.

How Do You Build Remarkability Into a Product?

The most important idea in the book is that marketing is not something you do after the product is finished. The product itself is the marketing. If the product is ordinary, no amount of clever advertising will make it remarkable. If the product is remarkable, the marketing largely takes care of itself.

Godin’s examples from the book’s era show how this works in practice. Some companies made products remarkable by design — so distinctive that people photographed them and showed friends. Others changed the experience around the product: a store layout nobody had seen before, a returns policy so generous it became a talking point, a service so fast it seemed impossible. In every case, the remarkability was a deliberate design decision, not a marketing afterthought.

For a small business, the lesson is liberating. You do not need a large advertising budget to be remarkable. You need one thing about your product or service that is genuinely different and genuinely worth talking about — a single feature, a single policy, or a single moment in the customer experience. One remarkable element, done well, is worth more than a dozen small improvements nobody notices.

The discipline is in choosing what to be remarkable at. A business cannot be remarkable at everything, and trying to be is a quick way to be remarkable at nothing. The right question is not “how can we be better?” but “what is the one thing people will tell their friends about?”

Remarkable is a design decision, not a marketing budget

You cannot advertise an ordinary product into remarkability. The purple cow has to be built into the product itself — one feature, policy, or experience so different that customers talk about it without being asked.

Is Playing It Safe Really the Riskiest Strategy?

Godin’s most provocative claim is that safety is the real risk. In a crowded market, a safe product is guaranteed to be ignored. It will not be talked about, it will not be shared, and it will not be remembered. The company that plays it safe has not avoided risk — it has chosen the certainty of being invisible over the uncertainty of standing out.

This is a hard message for large organisations, because their incentives point the other way. A manager who approves a remarkable product that fails can be blamed; one who approves an average product that fails can say they followed the standard process. The book argues that this logic is backwards: the standard process is precisely what produces invisible products.

There is an honest caveat. Being remarkable is not a guarantee of success. Some remarkable products fail because they are remarkable in the wrong way — strange without being useful, or different without being better. Remarkability gets attention, but the product still has to deliver value once people look.

Still, the asymmetry is real. An ordinary product needs a huge marketing budget to be noticed at all, and even then it is easily forgotten. A remarkable product earns attention for free, from the people who encounter it. In a world where attention is the scarcest resource, that is the better bet.

What Can Small Businesses Take From Purple Cow?

The book’s advice is especially useful for small businesses, because it does not require money — it requires courage. A small business cannot outspend a large competitor on advertising, but it can out-differentiate one. A local cafe can be remarkable with a single unforgettable dish; a small shop can be remarkable with a returns policy that seems almost reckless.

The practical steps are straightforward. First, look at what everyone in your market does the same way, and ask whether the sameness is a rule or just a habit. Second, pick one thing to do differently — one thing your best customers would describe to a friend. Third, tell the story of that difference everywhere. Remarkability only works if people can find it and repeat it.

Finally, accept that not everyone will like it. A purple cow is remarkable precisely because it is not for everyone. The customers who love it will love it loudly, and their enthusiasm is worth more than the approval of the indifferent majority.

Common Questions

Does a purple cow have to be weird?

No. Remarkable means worth making a remark about, and that can come from generosity, speed, simplicity, or beauty just as easily as from strangeness. Weirdness that serves no one is not a purple cow — it is a gimmick.

Is Purple Cow still relevant in the age of social media?

More than ever. Social media made word of mouth faster and more powerful, which is exactly the mechanism the book describes. A remarkable product can now spread to millions of people in days, while an ordinary product is filtered out by algorithms and ignored by users.

What’s the difference between remarkable and just different?

Difference is a fact; remarkability is an effect. A product can be different in ways nobody cares about — a different shade of packaging, a different font on the label. Remarkable means the difference is large enough that people talk about it.

Can a boring product be remarkable?

Yes. Godin’s argument applies to any category. A boring product becomes remarkable through its experience: extraordinary service, a surprising guarantee, or radical transparency. The more boring the category, the easier it is to stand out.
