The Decoy Effect: How a Third Option Changes Your Choice

By the Mindfluence team · Updated November 2026 · Reading time: about 7 minutes

Imagine choosing between two phone plans: basic for $30 a month and premium for $60. You pick basic — it covers what you need. Then the company adds a third plan: mid-tier at $55 with fewer features than premium. Suddenly the $60 premium plan looks like a bargain, and you switch to it. Nothing about the two original plans changed. The only change was a third option that nobody in their right mind would choose. That is the decoy effect, and it quietly shapes menus, pricing pages, and store shelves everywhere.

Key Facts at a Glance

  • The decoy effect — also called asymmetric dominance — is the shift in preference between two options when a third, inferior option is added.
  • It was first demonstrated in consumer research by Joel Huber, John Payne, and Christopher Puto in 1982.
  • The decoy must be clearly worse than one option (the target) but not worse than the other — that asymmetry steers the choice.
  • Dan Ariely’s famous Economist example showed a print-only subscription acting as a decoy that pushed readers toward the print-plus-web bundle.
  • Decoys appear in cinema popcorn sizes, phone storage tiers, streaming plans, and software pricing pages.

What Is the Decoy Effect?

The decoy effect describes what happens when adding a third option changes how you choose between two existing ones. Standard economic theory says this should not happen: an irrelevant new option should not change your preference between options you already had. But in practice, people do change their minds — in a predictable direction.

Suppose you are choosing between two restaurants: one is a five-minute walk away with good food, the other a twenty-minute drive away with excellent food. You are torn. Then a friend mentions a third: a twenty-five-minute drive away with merely decent food. Nobody would pick that third place — it is worse than the excellent restaurant on every count. But its presence makes the excellent restaurant look better: same long drive, much better food. Suddenly it feels like the obvious choice. The third restaurant was the decoy. It was never meant to be chosen — it was there to make its neighbour look good.

The technical name is asymmetric dominance: the decoy is dominated by one option — worse than it in every way — but not dominated by the other. That asymmetry is the whole trick. It gives your brain an easy comparison, and easy comparisons feel like good decisions.

Where Did the Idea Come From?

The decoy effect was first demonstrated by Joel Huber, John Payne, and Christopher Puto in a 1982 paper in the Journal of Consumer Research, “Adding Asymmetrically Dominated Alternatives: Violations of Regularity and the Similarity Hypothesis.” Their experiments showed that adding an option worse than one existing option, but not worse than the other, increased the share of people choosing the option that dominated it. The finding violated a basic assumption of rational choice theory — the regularity principle, which says that adding an option cannot increase the probability of choosing one already in the set. The decoy did exactly that.

The effect became widely known through Dan Ariely’s 2008 book Predictably Irrational, which described a real subscription offer from The Economist. The magazine offered three options: web-only for $59 a year, print-only for $125, and print-plus-web for $125. The print-only option was the decoy: it cost the same as the bundle but offered less — nobody sensible would choose print alone when print-plus-web cost exactly the same. Yet its presence changed everything.

Ariely tested the offer on his MIT students. With all three options shown, 16 percent chose the $59 web-only subscription and 84 percent chose the $125 bundle — nobody chose the print-only decoy. With the decoy removed, the split flipped: 68 percent chose web-only and only 32 percent chose the bundle. The decoy had more than doubled the expensive option’s share. The Economist presumably knew exactly what it was doing: the print-only option existed to make the bundle look like the smart choice.

Why Does the Decoy Effect Work?

The decoy effect works because people are much better at comparing than at judging in absolute terms. Tell someone a coffee costs $5 and ask if that is a good price, and they will struggle — good compared to what? But show them two coffees side by side, one $5 and one $7, and the $5 one suddenly looks like a deal. We think in relative terms, and the decoy hands us a flattering comparison.

The decoy also simplifies a hard decision. Choosing between two genuinely different options — cheap and basic versus expensive and feature-rich — means weighing trade-offs, and trade-offs are mentally tiring. The decoy offers a shortcut: one option is clearly better than another on every dimension, so the choice feels settled without the hard thinking. Your brain rewards that feeling of clarity, and you walk away confident in a rational pick. The irony is that the clarity was manufactured.

The effect is strongest when the choice is difficult — when the two main options are close in appeal and hard to compare directly. When one option is obviously better, no decoy is needed. It is precisely in the hard cases, where you most want a tiebreaker, that the decoy does its quiet work.

Where Will You See Decoys in Real Life?

Cinema popcorn is the classic example. A small might cost $3 and a large $7. Then the cinema adds a medium at $6.50 — only 50 cents less than the large. Almost nobody wants the medium: for 50 cents more you get the large. But the medium makes the large look like a bargain, and far more people buy the large than if only small and large were on the menu. The medium exists to sell the large.

Phone makers use the same structure with storage tiers: 128 GB, 256 GB, and 512 GB, with the middle tier priced close to the top one. The middle tier is the decoy — it makes the jump to the top tier feel small, so buyers who would have settled for the base model stretch to the most expensive one.

Subscription pricing pages are full of decoys too. A software company offers Basic, Standard, and Premium plans, with Standard priced just below Premium while offering noticeably less — fewer users, less storage, no priority support. Visitors compare Standard with Premium, conclude Premium is the better value, and sign up for the most expensive tier. The company never expected many people to buy Standard. It expected Standard to sell Premium.

Decoys appear in property listings too. An agent might show you two houses, then a third that is clearly worse than one of them — same price, smaller garden, longer commute. The third house makes its neighbour look like the catch of the year. Once you know the pattern, you will spot it in menus, catalogues, and comparison tables almost everywhere.

The removal test

When one option suddenly looks like an obvious bargain, try removing the weakest option from the list and choosing again. If your pick changes, a decoy was doing the deciding — not your actual preferences.

How Can You Avoid Being Steered by a Decoy?

The best defence is to decide what you need before you look at the options. If you know you need 128 GB of storage, the 256 GB decoy cannot push you to 512 GB. If you only watch two streaming shows a month, the premium bundle’s extra channels are worth nothing to you, however good the comparison looks. A clear sense of your own needs is the decoy’s worst enemy: the effect depends on comparing options instead of comparing each one against what you actually want.

Second, judge each option against your budget and your needs, not just against the other options on the page. Ask “is this worth $60 a month to me?” rather than “is this worth $60 when the other one is $55?” The first question is about value. The second is about the decoy.

Third, watch for the tell-tale structure: three options where the middle one is priced close to the top one but offers clearly less. When you see that pattern, you are almost certainly looking at a decoy. The middle option is not a real choice; it is a stage prop. Ignore it and choose between the remaining two on their own merits.

For businesses, the lesson runs the other way. The decoy effect is a legitimate pricing tool — used openly, it presents options in a way that highlights genuine value. But a decoy that misleads customers into buying something they do not need will eventually cost the business more in refunds, complaints, and lost trust than it ever gained in upsells. The best use of the decoy is to help customers see the value of the option that genuinely fits them best.

Common Questions

Is the decoy effect the same as anchoring?

No, though they often work together. Anchoring is when the first number you see shapes what feels reasonable — a $200 shirt makes a $100 shirt look cheap. The decoy effect is about a third option changing your preference between two others. A high-priced decoy can also act as an anchor, but the mechanisms differ: anchoring shifts your sense of value, while the decoy shifts your choice by making one option easy to compare favourably.

Does the decoy effect work on everyone?

It works on most people, but not equally. The effect is strongest when the choice is difficult and the options are hard to compare directly. People with strong prior preferences, or plenty of time and expertise, are less swayed. But even experts are not immune — the effect has been demonstrated in choices ranging from consumer products to political candidates and restaurants.

Can a decoy backfire on the seller?

Yes. If the decoy is too obviously bad, customers may notice the manipulation and lose trust. If it is priced too close to the target, some customers will pick the decoy itself. And if customers feel pushed into the expensive option and later regret it, they may return the product or cancel. A decoy works best when it highlights a genuinely better option, not when it tricks people into one.

How is a decoy different from a bad deal?

A bad deal is simply poor value — an option nobody should choose. A decoy is a bad deal placed deliberately next to a specific target to make that target look better. The difference is intent and placement: the Economist’s print-only option sat beside the bundle at the same price, existing only to make the bundle look like the obvious choice.
