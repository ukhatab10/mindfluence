Blue Ocean Strategy: How to Stop Competing and Create New Demand

By the Mindfluence team · Updated November 2026 · Reading time: about 7 minutes

Most businesses spend their lives fighting over the same customers. They cut prices, match features, and copy each other’s moves, until the whole market is exhausted and nobody makes much money. In 2005, two professors at the INSEAD business school, W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne, gave this trap a name — the red ocean — and proposed an alternative. Instead of fighting rivals for existing demand, they argued, companies should create new demand in uncontested market space: a blue ocean. Their book, Blue Ocean Strategy, became one of the most influential business books of its era.

Key Facts at a Glance

  • Blue Ocean Strategy is a book by W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne, published in 2005 by Harvard Business School Press.
  • “Red oceans” are crowded markets where rivals fight over the same demand, driving down prices and profits.
  • “Blue oceans” are uncontested new market space, created by offering something that makes the competition irrelevant.
  • The cornerstone of the approach is value innovation: raising value for buyers while simultaneously lowering costs.
  • The book’s main tool is the four actions framework: eliminate, reduce, raise, and create.

What Are Red Oceans and Blue Oceans?

The metaphor is vivid. A red ocean is a market where the water is stained by competition: every company is fighting the same rivals for the same customers, and the fighting itself destroys value. Prices fall, margins shrink, and products converge until they are nearly identical. Most industries, most of the time, are red oceans.

A blue ocean is the opposite: market space that nobody is fighting over, because nobody has claimed it yet. It is created, not found. A company creates a blue ocean by offering something that existing customers never asked for, or by reaching people who were never customers at all. The competition becomes irrelevant, not because it has been beaten, but because the game has changed.

Kim and Mauborgne’s research, which studied companies across many industries over more than a century, found that the businesses that created blue oceans were the ones that produced the most lasting growth. Red-ocean wins are usually temporary — the next price cut or feature match erases them — while a blue ocean can sustain a company for years, because there is nobody else in the water.

It is important to be clear about what a blue ocean is not. It is not a niche. A niche is a small slice of an existing market, served more precisely than the mainstream. A blue ocean is new demand — customers who did not buy from your industry before. A niche is still red water, just a quieter corner of it.

What Is Value Innovation?

The cornerstone of blue ocean strategy is an idea the authors call value innovation. Traditional strategy forces a choice: offer more value at a higher cost, or lower costs by offering less value. You can be the premium option or the cheap option, but not both. Value innovation breaks that trade-off by pursuing differentiation and low cost at the same time.

How is that possible? By questioning which features actually matter. Every industry accumulates features and costs that customers no longer value. A value innovator eliminates or reduces those, saving money, and spends the savings on the things buyers genuinely care about. The result is a product that is both cheaper to provide and more valuable to the customer — a combination competitors, locked into their cost structures, struggle to match.

Cirque du Soleil is the book’s central example of value innovation at work, and we look at it in detail below. The pattern is the same in every case: the innovator does not try to be better at the industry’s existing game. It changes which game is being played.

The Four Actions Framework: Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, Create

The book’s main practical tool is the four actions framework: four questions that force a company to rethink its offering from scratch, answered together, because the power of the framework comes from the combination.

Eliminate: which factors that the industry takes for granted should be eliminated? These are the features or costs that everyone in the industry provides, but that customers do not actually value. Eliminating them saves money and clears space for what matters.

Reduce: which factors should be reduced well below the industry standard? These are things customers value a little, but not as much as the industry assumes. Reducing them frees up resources without losing the core of the offering.

Raise: which factors should be raised well above the industry standard? These are the things customers genuinely care about, where the industry has been under-delivering.

Create: which factors should be created that the industry has never offered? This is the question that opens the blue ocean. The new factors attract customers who never bought from the industry before, because they offer something the old market never provided.

Working through all four questions produces an offering that looks nothing like the industry standard. If the answers leave you with something that resembles what your competitors already sell, you have not gone far enough — you are still swimming in red water.

How Did Cirque du Soleil Reinvent the Circus?

The circus industry in the early 1980s was a textbook red ocean. Audiences were shrinking, animal-rights campaigns were hurting the traditional shows, and the biggest circuses were competing by offering ever more spectacular — and expensive — versions of the same acts.

Cirque du Soleil, founded in Canada in 1984, changed the game entirely. It eliminated the two most expensive elements of the traditional circus: animal acts and star performers. Animals were costly to keep, transport, and insure, and increasingly unpopular with audiences. Star performers commanded high fees and could leave for a rival. Dropping both cut costs dramatically.

At the same time, Cirque raised and created. It raised the artistry: the performances became theatrical productions with original music, choreography, and a story running through the show. And it created something the circus had never offered — sophisticated entertainment aimed at adults, with ticket prices closer to theatre than to a travelling fair.

The result was a new kind of audience. People who would never have gone to a traditional circus — adults without children, theatre lovers, corporate clients — became Cirque customers. The company was not competing with other circuses for the same shrinking audience. It had created a blue ocean of demand that had not existed before.

A blue ocean is not a niche — it is new demand

A niche serves a small slice of an existing market better than the mainstream. A blue ocean creates customers who did not buy from your industry before. Cirque du Soleil did not win a bigger share of circus-goers; it attracted people who never went to the circus at all.

What Can the Nintendo Wii Teach Us?

A second famous example comes from video games. By the mid-2000s, the console market was a fierce red ocean. Sony and Microsoft were competing on processing power, graphics, and realistic games aimed at young male players. Each new console was more powerful and more expensive than the last.

Nintendo took a different path with the Wii, released in 2006. Instead of competing on graphics and power, it eliminated the race for raw performance and created something the industry had never offered: motion controls that anyone could pick up in seconds. Playing tennis meant swinging your arm; bowling meant rolling an imaginary ball.

The effect was to reach an audience the games industry had largely ignored: parents, grandparents, families, and people who had never played a video game. The Wii became a fixture in living rooms and even in retirement homes, because it offered social, physical fun rather than technical spectacle. Nintendo had not won the graphics war. It had walked away from it and created new demand instead.

Yellow Tail, an Australian wine brand, offers a smaller-scale version of the same pattern. The wine industry competed on complexity — heritage, terroir, and vintages that intimidated casual drinkers. Yellow Tail simplified everything: easy-drinking wines, bright labels, and a small, clear range. The blue ocean was not better wine. It was wine for people who had never bought wine.

How Do You Start Finding Your Own Blue Ocean?

The book’s method starts with looking outside your industry. Kim and Mauborgne argue that the best blue ocean ideas come from studying the people who do not buy from you — the non-customers — rather than surveying the customers you already have. Only non-customers can tell you why they stay away, and what would bring them in.

Start with three questions. First, who are the people who should be your customers but are not, and what keeps them away? Second, what does your industry take for granted that those people do not value? Third, what do those people value that your industry has never offered? The answers point toward the eliminate, reduce, raise, and create moves that define a blue ocean.

It is also worth being honest about the risks. Blue oceans do not stay blue forever. Success attracts imitators, and over time the new market becomes crowded — a blue ocean gradually turns red. The book’s answer is that companies should keep creating: a blue ocean is not a permanent position but a way of thinking.

For a small business, the lesson is practical rather than grand. You do not need to reinvent an industry to use blue ocean thinking. Find one group of people your competitors are ignoring, and one offering that serves them in a way nobody else does. That is a blue ocean at human scale.

Common Questions

Is blue ocean strategy just about being different?

No. Being different is not enough — the difference has to create new demand. A blue ocean requires value innovation: something buyers genuinely want, delivered at a cost structure competitors cannot easily match.

Do blue oceans stay blue forever?

No. Success attracts competitors, and over time a blue ocean becomes crowded and turns red. The Wii’s motion controls were copied, and Cirque du Soleil eventually faced rivals. The strategy is a discipline, not a one-time move.

Can small businesses use blue ocean strategy?

Yes, and it may suit them better than large companies. A small business cannot win a price war against a giant, but it can find a group of non-customers the giant is ignoring and serve them in a new way.

What’s the difference between blue ocean strategy and Purple Cow?

They are close cousins. Seth Godin’s Purple Cow is about being remarkable enough that people talk about you. Blue ocean strategy is about creating uncontested market space. In practice, the same product is often both: Cirque du Soleil was remarkable and it created a blue ocean.
