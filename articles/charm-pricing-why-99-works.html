Charm Pricing: Why $9.99 Feels Cheaper Than $10

By the Mindfluence team · Updated November 2026 · Reading time: about 7 minutes

Walk into almost any store and you will see the same pattern: $4.99, $19.99, $99.99. Prices ending in 9 are everywhere, and they have been for more than a century. The difference between $9.99 and $10.00 is a single cent — too small to matter to any household budget. Yet shoppers treat the two prices as if they belonged in different leagues. The $9.99 item feels like a deal; the $10.00 item feels like ten dollars. This is charm pricing, and the psychology behind it is surprisingly deep.

Key Facts at a Glance

  • Charm pricing means setting prices just below a round number — $9.99 instead of $10 — so the price feels noticeably cheaper than it really is.
  • The main explanation is the left-digit effect: shoppers focus on the leftmost digit, so $9.99 reads as “nine-something” while $10 reads as “ten.”
  • Thomas and Morwitz (2005) showed that the left-digit effect biases how shoppers judge the size of a price and the size of a discount.
  • Schindler and Kibarian (1996) found that prices ending in 99 were associated with low prices and increased sales in a retail test.
  • The practice dates back to 19th-century retailers, who used odd prices partly to force clerks to give change — and record the sale.

What Is Charm Pricing?

Charm pricing is the practice of setting a price just below a round number: $9.99 instead of $10, $49 instead of $50, $999 instead of $1,000. The name comes from the idea that the price has a kind of charm — it looks smaller than it is. The most common charm ending is 9, though 5 and 7 are also used. Walk down any supermarket aisle and count the prices ending in 9. In many stores they outnumber every other ending combined.

The puzzle is why it works at all. Every shopper knows $9.99 is one cent less than $10, and nobody believes the two prices are far apart. Yet study after study finds that charm prices sell more than round prices for the same product. The effect is small for any single purchase, but across millions of transactions it is worth a fortune — which is why retailers have used it for over a hundred years.

Part of the answer is that shoppers do not read prices the way they read other numbers. A price is not processed digit by digit with careful arithmetic. It is scanned quickly, judged roughly, and filed away as a feeling: cheap, fair, or expensive. Charm pricing works on that quick, rough judgement — not on the careful arithmetic that comes later, if it comes at all.

Why Does $9.99 Feel Cheaper? The Left-Digit Effect

The best-supported explanation is the left-digit effect. When we read a number from left to right, the first digit carries the most weight in our judgement of how big the number is. The price $9.99 starts with a 9; the price $10.00 starts with a 1 followed by a 0, which our brains register as “ten.” The gap between “nine-something” and “ten” feels much larger than one cent, because the leftmost digit has changed — and with it, the whole category the price belongs to. Nine dollars is single-digit territory; ten dollars is double-digit territory. That boundary feels meaningful even though the actual difference is trivial.

This effect was demonstrated in a 2005 paper by Manoj Thomas and Vicki Morwitz in the Journal of Consumer Research, titled “Penny Wise and Pound Foolish: The Left-Digit Effect in Price Cognition.” Their experiments showed that shoppers judge the difference between prices like $2.99 and $3.00 as larger than the difference between $2.00 and $2.01 — even though both gaps are a single cent. The change in the leftmost digit exaggerates the perceived gap. The same bias affects how shoppers judge discounts: a price cut from $3.00 to $2.99 feels bigger than a cut from $2.01 to $2.00, because the first cut changes the leftmost digit and the second does not.

There is a second mechanism at work too: association. Over a lifetime of shopping, we learn that prices ending in 99 usually belong to bargains, discounts, and value ranges. The ending itself becomes a signal. A price of $19.99 does not just look smaller than $20.00 — it looks like the kind of price a store sets when it wants to signal value. That learned association reinforces the left-digit effect, and together they make the charm price feel like a deal before any real comparison happens.

What Does the Research Say?

One of the most cited studies of charm pricing comes from Robert Schindler and Thomas Kibarian, published in the Journal of Retailing in 1996. In a test with a women’s clothing retailer, they compared sales of the same items at prices ending in 99 with sales at nearby round or 00-ending prices. The 99-ending prices were associated with low prices in shoppers’ minds, and the items sold better at the 99-ending prices. The study helped establish that the effect is real in actual stores, not just in laboratory experiments — shoppers really do buy more when the price ends in 99.

Later research has added nuance. The left-digit effect explains much of it, but not everything. Charm prices work best when shoppers browse quickly or compare many items — exactly the conditions in a supermarket or on a shopping app. When shoppers slow down and compare carefully, the effect weakens, though it rarely disappears entirely.

Researchers have also found that the charm effect depends on the price range. The jump from $9.99 to $10.00 crosses a round-number boundary, so the left-digit change is large and the effect is strong. The jump from $9.49 to $9.50 changes no left digit, so the effect is weak. This is why retailers cluster their prices just below round numbers — $9.99, $19.99, $49.99 — rather than at random odd endings. The magic is in the digit that changes, not in the 9 itself.

Where Did Charm Pricing Come From?

Charm pricing is older than modern psychology. The practice took hold among retailers in the 19th century, and the commonly told origin story is a practical one. In the days before cash registers, a shop assistant selling an item for exactly $1.00 could pocket the coin and never record the sale. But an item priced at 99 cents forced the assistant to open the till, give the customer a cent in change, and record the transaction — making theft much harder. Whether or not this was the main reason, the practice stuck, and retailers soon noticed that the odd prices also seemed to sell more goods.

By the early 20th century, charm pricing was standard practice in department stores and mail-order catalogues, and it has never really gone away. The rise of e-commerce has only strengthened it: online shoppers scan dozens of prices in seconds, exactly the conditions under which the left-digit effect is strongest. App stores are full of $0.99, $4.99, and $9.99 price points, and streaming services cluster at $9.99 and $14.99 a month. The 19th-century trick survives because the psychology behind it has not changed.

The round-up test

When comparing prices, round every charm price up to the nearest dollar in your head: $9.99 becomes $10, $19.99 becomes $20. Compare the rounded numbers instead. If the “deal” disappears when you do, it was the left digit talking — not the price.

When Does Charm Pricing Not Work?

Charm pricing is not a universal trick. It works best for value and mid-range products, where shoppers are looking for a good deal. For luxury goods, it can backfire. A handbag priced at $1,999 signals a bargain; the same bag at $2,000 signals prestige. Luxury brands usually prefer round numbers precisely because round prices feel deliberate, confident, and premium, while 99-endings feel like a discount rack. Research on price endings supports this: consumers associate 99-endings with low prices, and round endings with quality.

Charm pricing also loses power when shoppers are highly involved. Buying a car or a house, people compare carefully, negotiate, and do the arithmetic — a $19,999 sticker price fools nobody signing a five-year loan. And repeated exposure dulls the effect: shoppers who see 99-endings on every shelf may stop registering them as special. The trick still works on average, but it is a nudge, not a spell.

For businesses, the lesson is to match the price ending to the positioning. A discount store should lean into 99-endings — they reinforce the value message. A premium brand should think twice: the same ending that boosts sales of a $9.99 phone case can cheapen a $2,000 watch. And for shoppers, the lesson is simpler: round the price up in your head, and judge the product on what it is worth to you, not on which digit its price starts with.

Common Questions

Does $9.99 really sell better than $10?

In retail tests, yes — items priced just below a round number generally sell better than the same items at the round price. Schindler and Kibarian’s 1996 retail test found that 99-ending prices increased sales, and the left-digit effect documented by Thomas and Morwitz (2005) explains why: shoppers judge the gap between $9.99 and $10.00 as larger than one cent because the leftmost digit changes.

Why 9 and not 8 or 7?

The digit 9 is the largest single digit, so a price ending in 9 sits as close as possible to the round number above it while keeping the lower left digit — $9.99 keeps the “9” while capturing nearly all of $10. Other endings like 5 and 7 are used too, but 9 maximises the gap between the perceived price and the actual price, which is why it dominates store shelves.

Is charm pricing dishonest?

Most people would say no — the price is displayed openly, and every shopper can see the full number. Charm pricing exploits a quirk in how brains process numbers, but it does not hide information or mislead about the product. It becomes a problem only when combined with genuinely deceptive practices, such as fake “was” prices or hidden fees.

Do charm prices work for services and subscriptions too?

Yes. Streaming services, apps, gyms, and software subscriptions routinely use $4.99, $9.99, and $14.99 monthly prices. The same left-digit effect applies: $9.99 a month feels like single-digit spending, while $10 a month feels like a double-digit commitment. Over a year, the difference is only 12 cents — but the feeling of the price shapes the sign-up decision.
