The Sunk Cost Fallacy: Why We Keep Paying for Things That Don’t Work

By the Mindfluence team · Updated November 2026 · Reading time: about 7 minutes

You have sat through a terrible movie because you paid for the ticket. You have kept a gym membership you never use because cancelling would mean admitting the money was wasted. You have stayed in a project long after it stopped making sense, because so much had already gone into it. Every one of these is the sunk cost fallacy at work: the habit of letting money, time, or effort already spent — money that is gone whatever you do — dictate what you do next. Economists have known for decades that sunk costs should be ignored. Psychologists have shown, just as clearly, that people almost never ignore them.

Key Facts at a Glance

  • A sunk cost is money, time, or effort already spent that cannot be recovered, whatever you decide next.
  • The sunk cost fallacy is letting those unrecoverable costs influence a forward-looking decision — staying in a bad movie because the ticket is paid for.
  • Economist Richard Thaler described the effect in 1980, in a paper arguing that real consumers do not behave the way standard economic theory predicts.
  • In a classic 1985 experiment by Arkes and Blumer, theatregoers who paid full price for season tickets attended more shows than those who paid less or got tickets free.
  • The Concorde supersonic airliner gave the fallacy its most famous nickname: governments kept funding it largely because so much had already been spent.

What Is a Sunk Cost?

A sunk cost is any cost that is already paid and cannot be got back. The movie ticket you bought is a sunk cost once the film starts. The year you spent learning a skill is a sunk cost. The money a company poured into a product that flopped is a sunk cost. The defining feature is simple: no decision you make now can recover it.

Standard economics has a clear rule about sunk costs: ignore them. A rational decision looks only forward, comparing the future costs and future benefits of each option. What you already spent is irrelevant, because it is the same in every scenario. If the movie is bad, the only question is whether the next hour is better spent in the cinema or elsewhere.

Real people do not decide this way. We treat past spending as a reason to keep going, as though abandoning something makes the earlier spending a waste, while persisting somehow rescues it. It does not. The money is gone either way, but the feeling that quitting “wastes” the investment drives an enormous amount of bad spending, in households and in boardrooms alike.

The Theatre Ticket Experiment That Proved It

The clearest early demonstration came from psychologists Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer, working at Ohio University. In 1985 they ran a field experiment with season tickets for the university theatre. Some ticket holders had paid the full price of $15 per ticket. Others had been offered a discount and paid $13. A third group had received their tickets free.

The researchers then tracked how many of the season’s plays each group actually attended. The full-price group went to significantly more shows than either of the other groups. The plays were the same, the seats were the same, and the tickets were already paid for — so the cost of attending any given show was identical for everyone. The only difference was how much each person had originally paid. The more they had sunk into the tickets, the more determined they were to use them.

The experiment matters because it was not a survey or a hypothetical scenario. It measured real behaviour, with real money, over a whole theatre season — sunk costs distort what people actually do, not just what they say they would do.

Arkes and Blumer’s finding built on work by the economist Richard Thaler, who had argued in a landmark 1980 paper that consumers systematically violate the rules of standard economic theory — they treat sunk costs as relevant to decisions, and a realistic theory of consumer choice had to account for that. The paper helped launch behavioural economics, and Thaler later won the Nobel Prize in Economics for this body of work.

Why Does Our Brain Fall for It?

The sunk cost fallacy survives because it is fed by several powerful feelings at once. The first is loss aversion: losses hurt more than equivalent gains feel good, so walking away from something you paid for feels like accepting a loss, even when staying costs more.

The second is the desire to appear consistent — to ourselves and to others. Admitting a project was a mistake means admitting the original decision was wrong. Persisting lets us maintain the story that the decision was sound. Psychologists call this self-justification, and it is strongest when the original decision was public, expensive, or made with confidence.

The third is a simple discomfort with waste. Abandoning something half-used feels wasteful in a way that is hard to shake. The irony is that this dislike of waste often produces more waste: more hours in a bad movie, more months of an unused membership, more years in a failing project. The effort to avoid wasting the past ends up wasting the future.

Where Does the Sunk Cost Fallacy Show Up?

In everyday life, the fallacy is almost a routine. The unused gym membership is the textbook case: the monthly fee keeps coming out of the account, and cancelling feels like admitting the previous payments were wasted — so the payments continue, and the waste grows. Streaming subscriptions work the same way. Many people keep three or four services they barely watch, because each one feels like a small commitment already made.

Relationships and careers are harder cases of the same pattern. People stay in unhappy relationships because of the years already invested, and stay in careers they dislike because of the degree or the decade already spent. The years are sunk either way; the only real question is whether the next year is better spent where you are or somewhere else.

Businesses fall for it at a much larger scale. A company that has spent millions developing a product will often keep funding it long after the market has spoken, because cancelling means writing off the investment. Investors hold losing stocks for the same reason: selling locks in the loss, while holding keeps the hope alive that the price will come back.

Marketers understand the fallacy very well. Free trials that ask for a card upfront, loyalty programmes that reward you for staying, and setup processes that take hours all exploit the same instinct: once you have invested something, leaving feels expensive. Knowing the mechanism is the best defence against it.

The question that cuts through it

When you are deciding whether to keep going, ask: “If I had spent nothing so far, would I still choose this?” If the answer is no, the past spending is making the decision for you — and the past spending is the one thing that should not.

The Concorde Fallacy: Sunk Costs at National Scale

The most famous example of the sunk cost fallacy is so well known that it gave the bias a second name. Concorde, the supersonic passenger jet developed jointly by Britain and France, was a technological marvel and a commercial failure. Development costs ballooned far beyond the original estimates, and it became clear relatively early that the aircraft would never make back its investment.

Yet both governments kept funding it for years, on the reasoning that so much money had already been spent that stopping would waste it all. The biologist Richard Dawkins and his colleague R. Carlisle gave this pattern its name in a 1976 paper, calling it the “Concorde fallacy”: the mistake of continuing an endeavour because of past investment rather than future prospects.

Concorde did eventually fly, and it flew for 27 years as a beloved symbol of engineering ambition. But as a business decision, the continued funding is the classic case study: two governments, advised by the best economists of the day, fell into the same trap as a person sitting through a bad movie — only the ticket cost billions. The decision to continue should have been based on what Concorde would earn from that point forward, not on what had already been spent. Past spending is a fact, not an argument.

How Do You Beat the Sunk Cost Fallacy?

The first step is naming it. Simply recognising “this is a sunk cost” weakens its grip, because the fallacy works best when it is invisible. When you catch yourself saying “but I’ve already spent so much,” treat that sentence as a warning sign rather than a reason.

The second step is the reset question from the callout above: if you had spent nothing, would you start this today? Would you buy this ticket, sign up for this membership, fund this project, from scratch? If not, the honest move is to stop — not because the past spending was foolish, but because the future spending would be.

The third step is to separate the decision from the story. Quitting a project does not erase what you learned from it, and cancelling a membership does not make the months you did use it a waste. The value you already got is yours to keep; the fallacy only asks you to keep paying for value you are no longer getting.

Finally, set decision points in advance. Before starting a project, a subscription, or an investment, decide the conditions under which you will stop — a date, a budget, a result. Writing the exit rule down before you are emotionally invested makes it far easier to follow later.

Common Questions

Is finishing a bad movie really a sunk cost fallacy?

Yes, it is the classic everyday example. The ticket money is gone whether you stay or leave, so the only thing that matters is whether the remaining time is better spent in the cinema or elsewhere.

What’s the difference between sunk costs and commitment?

Commitment is forward-looking: you keep going because the future benefits justify the future costs. The sunk cost fallacy is backward-looking: you keep going because of what you already spent.

Does the sunk cost fallacy affect businesses too?

Very much so. Companies keep funding failing products, losing campaigns, and unprofitable divisions because of the money already invested. Concorde is the most famous example, but the pattern repeats in every industry.

How is it different from loss aversion?

Loss aversion is the general finding that losses feel worse than equivalent gains feel good. The sunk cost fallacy is one specific way loss aversion shows up: quitting feels like locking in a loss, so we keep going to avoid that feeling.
