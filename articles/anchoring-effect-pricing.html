The Anchoring Effect: How the First Price You See Changes What You’ll Pay

By the Mindfluence team · Updated November 2026 · Reading time: about 7 minutes

Walk into a store and the first price you see is $1,200 for a jacket. A few racks over, another jacket costs $400 — and it suddenly feels like a bargain. But is it? The $400 jacket did not change. What changed is the number your brain compared it with. Psychologists call this the anchoring effect: the first number we encounter pulls our judgment toward it, even when that number has nothing to do with the real value of what we are buying. Once you see it, you will notice it everywhere — on sale tags, in salary talks, and on car lots.

Key Facts at a Glance

  • Anchoring was first demonstrated by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman in 1974: people shown a random number from a rigged wheel gave very different estimates depending on the number they saw.
  • In that experiment, participants given the anchor 10 estimated a median of 25% for the share of African nations in the UN; those given 65 estimated a median of 45%.
  • In a 1987 study by Northcraft and Neale, experienced real estate agents valued the same house differently depending on its listing price.
  • Anchoring works even when people know the anchor is arbitrary — awareness alone does not switch it off.
  • Retailers use anchors everywhere: crossed-out “was” prices, the most expensive item shown first, and “compare at” prices.

What Is the Anchoring Effect?

The anchoring effect is a mental shortcut. When we have to judge a number — a fair price, a reasonable salary, the value of a house — we start from the first number that comes to mind and then adjust from there, but we almost never adjust far enough. The first number acts like an anchor: our thinking can drift, but it stays tethered to where the anchor landed.

This is not carelessness. When we do not know the true value of something, any number feels better than none, so the brain grabs the first one available as a starting point. That is why anchoring shows up in experts as well as beginners, and in high-stakes decisions as well as everyday shopping.

The Wheel-of-Fortune Experiment That Started It All

The classic demonstration comes from a 1974 paper by psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, published in Science. They asked participants what percentage of African nations belong to the United Nations — a question most people would have to guess at. Before answering, each participant spun a rigged wheel of fortune that stopped at either 10 or 65. The wheel was openly random: everyone could see it was a game of chance, not a source of information.

Then came the twist. Participants were first asked whether the true percentage was higher or lower than the number on the wheel, and then asked for their best estimate. The group that saw 10 gave a median estimate of 25%. The group that saw 65 gave a median estimate of 45%. A meaningless number, produced by a wheel everyone knew was random, moved people’s answers by twenty percentage points.

The result was startling because the anchor carried no information at all. Nobody believed the wheel knew anything about the United Nations, yet the number still pulled their judgment toward it. Later research found the same pattern with prices: in one study by Dan Ariely and colleagues, people shown the last two digits of their Social Security number offered more for a bottle of wine when the number was higher. The anchor does not need to make sense. It only needs to come first.

How Does Anchoring Show Up in Stores?

The most familiar anchor in retail is the crossed-out price. A tag reading “Was $120, now $60” sets $120 as the anchor, and $60 feels like a bargain by comparison. The trick is that the “was” price is often the highest price the item was ever listed at, sometimes briefly, and sometimes the item rarely sold at that price at all. Your brain still treats $120 as the reference point, and $60 as the deal of the day.

Online stores do the same with “compare at” or “list price” figures: “List price: $89.99, our price: $39.99” anchors you on $89.99 before you have decided whether the product is worth $40. The list price may be a figure no seller ever charges — but as an anchor, it still works.

Another common move is showing the most expensive option first. A restaurant puts a $90 bottle of wine at the top of the list, and the $45 bottle below it suddenly looks reasonable. A software company lists its $299-a-month plan first, and the $99 plan looks like the sensible middle choice. The expensive item is not necessarily there to sell; it is there to set the anchor.

Why Does the First Number Stick?

Psychologists offer two main explanations, and both are probably at work. The first is insufficient adjustment: we start at the anchor and move away from it, but we stop too soon. If the anchor is $1,200 and the true value is $300, we might adjust down to $700 and feel generous — even though we are still far above the real value. The adjustment feels big only because it is measured from the anchor, and the anchor was the wrong starting point.

The second explanation is that the anchor changes what comes to mind. When you hear a high price first, your brain starts looking for reasons the item might be worth that much: the quality, the brand, the craftsmanship. When you hear a low price first, your brain looks for reasons it might be cheap. The anchor quietly steers the evidence you consider, so your final judgment feels well-reasoned even though the starting point was arbitrary.

Both explanations point to the same conclusion: the anchor works before conscious thinking gets a chance. By the time you are weighing up a purchase, the first number has already framed the question — which is why simply knowing about anchoring is rarely enough to defeat it.

The anchor does not have to be true

The most striking part of the wheel experiment is that everyone knew the number was random — and it still worked. An anchor does not need to be accurate, fair, or even related to the decision. It only needs to be the first number you see.

Where Else Does Anchoring Show Up?

Anchoring is not just a shopping trick. In a 1987 study by Gregory Northcraft and Margaret Neale, experienced real estate agents appraised the same house after being given a high or a low listing price. Their valuations moved with the listing price, even though they had the same facts about the property — and even though they denied afterward that the listing price had influenced them. If trained experts can be anchored by a number they know is negotiable, ordinary buyers stand little chance without preparation.

Car dealerships run on anchors. The sticker price on the windshield is the opening anchor, and every discount from it feels like a win, even if the sticker price was inflated to begin with. Smart buyers research the market price before they walk in, so they carry their own anchor instead of accepting the dealer’s.

Salary negotiations work the same way. The first number named — whether by the employer or the candidate — sets the range for everything that follows. A candidate who names a figure first, backed by market research, anchors the discussion around their number; an employer who opens low anchors it around theirs. That is why career advisers tell job seekers to research salary ranges before any negotiation begins.

How Can You Stop an Anchor From Setting Your Price?

The best defence is to arrive with your own number. Before you shop for anything expensive, research the going rate: check what similar items sell for and decide in advance what the item is worth to you. A buyer with a researched price in mind is far harder to anchor than one walking in cold.

Second, judge every price on its own. When you see a crossed-out “was” price, ask yourself a simple question: would I pay this price if I had never seen the other number? If the answer is no, the discount is doing the persuading, not the value. The same test works for “compare at” prices and for the expensive item shown first on a menu or pricing page.

Third, in negotiations, be ready to name your own number — and to explain it. If the other side opens high, do not just haggle downward from their figure; state your own figure and the reasons behind it. A precise number signals that you have done your homework, and it is harder to dismiss.

Finally, slow down. Anchoring works best on fast, instinctive decisions, so a pause gives your more deliberate thinking a chance to question the first number. Step away from the shelf, close the tab, or sleep on it — the anchor loses much of its power once you stop reacting to it.

Common Questions

Does anchoring work even if you know about it?

Mostly, yes. In the original wheel experiment, participants could see the number was random, and it still pulled their estimates. The effect happens in the first moments of a decision, before deliberate thinking kicks in, so preparation — researching prices in advance — works better than willpower.

Is anchoring the same as a discount?

No. A discount changes what you pay; an anchor changes what you think something is worth. An anchor can make an ordinary price feel like a discount by placing a higher number next to it, which is why it pays to judge the final price on its own merits.

Can I use anchoring in a salary negotiation?

Yes, and it is one of the most practical uses of the effect. Research the market rate for the role, then name a specific figure at the top of the fair range, with reasons to back it up. Just make sure your number is defensible — an anchor that is wildly unrealistic can damage your credibility.

Why do stores put the most expensive item first?

Because it makes everything after it look cheaper. A $90 bottle of wine at the top of the list is the anchor; the $45 bottle below it feels sensible rather than a splurge. The expensive item may rarely sell, but it earns its place by shifting how customers see the rest of the range.
