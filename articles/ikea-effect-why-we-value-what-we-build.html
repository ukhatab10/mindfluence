The IKEA Effect: Why We Value Things We Build Ourselves

By the Mindfluence team · Updated November 2026 · Reading time: about 7 minutes

Think of the last piece of flat-pack furniture you put together. The instructions were confusing, you had one screw left over, and it took twice as long as promised. Yet when you stepped back, you probably felt a strange fondness for that wobbly bookshelf — far more than you would feel for the identical shelf bought ready-made. That feeling has a name: the IKEA effect. It is the tendency to place a higher value on things we helped create, and it shapes far more than furniture. From meal kits to custom sneakers, businesses have learned that a little well-placed effort can make customers love a product more.

Key Facts at a Glance

  • The IKEA effect is the tendency to value things more highly when you have put your own labour into making or assembling them.
  • It was named and tested by researchers Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon, and Dan Ariely in a 2012 paper, “The IKEA effect: When labor leads to love.”
  • In their experiments, people who assembled furniture themselves valued it significantly higher than identical pre-assembled furniture.
  • People also valued their own amateur origami creations far above what other people were willing to pay for them.
  • The effect disappears when the task is too difficult, or when the finished result is destroyed — effort alone is not enough.

What Is the IKEA Effect?

The IKEA effect is simple to state: we like things more when we have made them. The name comes from the Swedish furniture retailer whose business model is built on customers assembling their own bookcases, beds, and wardrobes. The company saves on manufacturing and shipping costs by selling flat boxes, and customers — perhaps surprisingly — end up valuing the finished product more than they would a ready-built equivalent.

It is not just about furniture. The same bias shows up whenever your own effort goes into the final result: a cake mixed from a box feels more like “yours” than one bought from a bakery, and a garden you planted yourself is worth more to you than the same plants bought fully grown. The labour itself becomes part of the product’s value in your mind.

Importantly, the IKEA effect is not the same as simply owning something. Researchers distinguish it from the endowment effect, where merely possessing an item raises its perceived value. The IKEA effect is specifically about labour: the value comes from the work you put in, not from the fact that the object sits in your home.

What Did the Original Experiments Actually Show?

The effect was formally demonstrated by Michael Norton of Harvard Business School, Daniel Mochon of Tulane University, and Dan Ariely of Duke University. Their paper, published in the Journal of Consumer Psychology in 2012, ran a series of experiments that isolated the role of labour in how we value things.

In one experiment, participants were asked to assemble simple IKEA storage boxes. Another group was shown identical boxes that were already assembled. When both groups were asked how much the boxes were worth, the builders valued their boxes significantly higher than the non-builders valued the pre-assembled ones. The objects were the same. The only difference was whose hands had put them together.

The most memorable experiment used origami. Participants folded paper frogs and cranes, then were asked how much they would pay for their own creation. Outside evaluators were asked the same question about the same amateur origami. The builders valued their own folded paper far above what anyone else would pay for it. They saw their clumsy frog through the lens of their own effort, and that lens was flattering.

Two further findings sharpened the picture. When participants were told their origami would be destroyed afterwards, the effect vanished — people did not value creations they knew would not survive. And when the folding task was made much harder, the effect also disappeared: people who struggled and produced a poor result did not treasure it. Effort only creates love when it leads to a result the person can feel good about.

Why Does Building Something Make Us Value It More?

Psychologists point to a few overlapping reasons. The first is effort justification: when we invest time and energy in something, our brains look for a reason that investment was worthwhile. Valuing the result more highly is a convenient answer. Admitting the bookshelf is mediocre after two hours of work would feel like admitting the two hours were wasted.

The second is a sense of competence. Completing a task — even a small one — gives us a feeling of capability. That warm feeling gets attached to the object itself. The bookshelf is not just a shelf; it is proof that you can follow instructions, use an Allen key, and finish what you started. The product becomes a small trophy.

The third is identity. Things we make feel like an extension of ourselves in a way that things we buy do not, which is why handmade gifts often feel more meaningful than expensive ones: the labour signals care, and the care becomes part of the object’s meaning. Marketers have noticed all of this, and the effect is now deliberately designed into products, apps, and services — which is where it pays to understand it.

Where Do Marketers Use the IKEA Effect?

Once you know what to look for, the IKEA effect is everywhere in modern marketing. Meal-kit companies are a classic example. They send pre-measured ingredients and a recipe card, and you do the cooking. The meal is not cheaper or easier than takeaway, yet customers describe the food as better and feel more attached to the brand. The cooking — the labour — is the point.

Customisation is another rich territory. Sneaker brands let you design your own colour combinations, furniture companies let you choose fabrics and finishes, and software tools let you build your own dashboard. Research on mass customisation has found that people value products they designed themselves more highly than equivalent off-the-shelf versions, even when the design choices are modest. Choosing is a form of labour, and it creates the same attachment.

Onboarding flows in apps use a gentler version of the same idea. When a fitness app asks you to set your own goals and log your first workout, it is not only gathering data — it is getting you to invest effort, and users who have set up an account themselves are more likely to stick with it, because the setup effort has made the account feel like theirs.

Even the humble “some assembly required” label is a deliberate choice. A small amount of assembly does two jobs at once: it lowers shipping costs, and it makes the customer value the product more. That is a rare win for both sides of the transaction.

Effort is the ingredient, not the product

The IKEA effect works because effort leads to a result you can be proud of. Make the task too hard, or destroy the result, and the effect disappears. The sweet spot is a task that is challenging enough to feel like an achievement, but easy enough to finish well.

When Does the IKEA Effect Disappear?

The limits of the effect matter as much as the effect itself. Norton, Mochon, and Ariely showed that the effect depends on successful completion. If the task is so hard that people fail, or produce something they are embarrassed by, there is no extra value — only frustration. A flat-pack wardrobe that collapses is not treasured; it is resented.

The effect also depends on the result surviving. When participants expected their creations to be destroyed, they did not value them more. The labour only counts when it produces something lasting. This is why the effect is strong for furniture and gardens, and weak for one-off tasks whose results vanish immediately.

There is also a limit on how much effort helps. A little effort creates attachment; a great deal of effort can create resentment. Customers who feel a company has pushed unpaid labour onto them — assembly that takes hours, a “simple” recipe with forty steps — do not feel love. They feel exploited.

How Can You Use It Without Manipulating People?

If you sell a product or run a business, the IKEA effect offers a genuine opportunity — and a genuine ethical line. The opportunity is to let customers participate in the final product in ways they enjoy: a choice of colours, a simple assembly step, a recipe they finish themselves, a workspace they arrange. When the participation is pleasant and the result is good, customers get a product they value more, and you get a customer who is more loyal. That is a fair exchange.

The line is crossed when effort is used to trap people. Making cancellation difficult, or forcing customers through tedious steps so they feel too invested to leave, is not the IKEA effect — it is manipulation dressed up as psychology. The difference is simple: does the effort make the product better for the customer, or does it only make leaving harder?

As a consumer, the same knowledge is protective. When you feel strangely attached to something you assembled, cooked, or customised, ask yourself a plain question: would I value this as much if someone else had made it? If the answer is no, your effort is doing the valuing, not the product. That does not mean the feeling is wrong — but it is worth knowing where it came from before you pay a premium for it, or refuse to replace something that no longer works.

Common Questions

Is the IKEA effect the same as the endowment effect?

They are related but different. The endowment effect is the tendency to value something more simply because you own it. The IKEA effect is specifically about labour: you value something more because you helped make it.

Does the IKEA effect work for digital products?

Yes. Customising a dashboard, building a playlist, or designing an avatar all involve effort, and people tend to value the result more than an identical pre-made version. The same limits apply: the task must be easy enough to complete successfully.

Why doesn’t the effect work when the task is too hard?

Because the value comes from successful effort, not from effort alone. In the original research, people who struggled through a difficult origami task and produced a poor result did not value their creation more.

Can companies overdo it?

Absolutely. A small, satisfying step — choosing a colour, assembling one part, finishing a recipe — creates attachment. Hours of frustrating assembly or a setup process that feels like unpaid work creates resentment instead.
